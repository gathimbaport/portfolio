Running a Construction Contract: A Practical Guide

John Wachira Gathimba • August 20, 2026 • 8 min read

A construction project does not succeed simply because the building is completed. Behind every successful project is a properly managed contract that establishes the responsibilities, rights and obligations of the parties involved.

Running a construction contract involves much more than checking whether work is progressing on site. It requires continuous management of time, cost, quality, payments, variations, instructions, documentation and communication between the client, consultant and contractor.

For Quantity Surveyors and other construction professionals, understanding how a contract operates throughout the construction period is therefore essential.

01. Understanding the Contract

Before construction begins, every member of the project team should understand the contract they are working under.

The contract establishes important matters such as:

  • The scope of works
  • The contract price
  • The construction period
  • Responsibilities of each party
  • Payment procedures
  • Variation procedures
  • Extension of time provisions
  • Defects liability provisions
  • Insurance requirements
  • Dispute resolution procedures
QS INSIGHT

The contract should not be treated as a document that only becomes important when a dispute occurs. It should guide the project team throughout the construction process.

02. Site Possession & Commencement

Once the contract has been executed, the project moves into the construction stage.

One of the important early events is the formal possession of the site. At this stage, the project team should establish the commencement date and contractual completion date.

These dates become important when assessing progress and determining whether delays have occurred.

03. Managing Valuations & Payments

One of the most important responsibilities during construction is ensuring that work executed by the contractor is properly measured and valued.

01 Inspect
02 Measure
03 Value
04 Certify

Accurate measurement is critical. If work is overvalued, the client may pay more than is justified. If it is undervalued, the contractor may experience unnecessary cash-flow problems.

04. Managing Variations

Changes to the original scope are common in construction. A client may request an additional facility, a consultant may revise a design, or unforeseen site conditions may require changes to the works.

INSTRUCTION → MEASUREMENT → VALUATION → APPROVAL

Every variation should be properly documented. The Quantity Surveyor should assess its financial effect and ensure that the valuation is consistent with the terms of the contract.

05. Monitoring Progress & Delays

Time is another major component of contract administration. The project team should continuously compare actual progress against the approved programme.

Delays can arise from late instructions, design changes, material shortages, poor contractor performance, adverse weather or unforeseen ground conditions.

IMPORTANT

Not every delay automatically gives the contractor an entitlement to additional time or money. The contract should be examined to determine the applicable provisions and procedures.

06. Claims & Disputes

Construction projects involve multiple parties, and disagreements can occur. Claims may relate to additional costs, delays, variations, loss and expense or other contractual entitlements.

Good contract administration maintains a clear record of instructions, site meetings, progress reports, measurements, payment certificates, variation orders, correspondence and programme updates.

07. Practical Completion

As the project approaches completion, the focus shifts towards inspection, testing and handover.

The consultant team should inspect the works and identify outstanding items or defects that need to be addressed.

08. The Final Account

The final account brings together the financial position of the project. It should account for the original contract sum together with approved changes and other contractual adjustments.

Depending on the contract, the final account may include:

  • Measured works
  • Variations
  • Provisional sums
  • Prime cost items
  • Dayworks
  • Claims
  • Approved adjustments
  • Retention
  • Other contractual deductions

09. The QS's Role

The Quantity Surveyor plays an important role in maintaining financial control throughout the project.

A GOOD QS COMBINES
Contract Knowledge + Measurement + Cost Management + Documentation + Communication

Conclusion

Running a construction contract is a continuous process that begins before construction and continues until the financial and contractual obligations of the project have been concluded.

Successful contract administration depends on understanding the contract, maintaining accurate records, monitoring cost and time, properly managing variations and ensuring that contractual procedures are followed.

The strongest projects are not necessarily those without problems. They are projects where problems are identified early, documented properly and managed according to the contract.
